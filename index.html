Bridgestone X-Form

Consolidating a fragmented multi-brand, multi-region estate onto one modern platform, with redesigned websites and mobile-first journeys, so 7 brands could ship once instead of rebuilding the same features again and again.

Role
Product Manager

Problem

Every brand, BU and region ran its own stack

Siloed stacks and clouds

Brands, BUs and regional teams ran disconnected platforms and cloud environments, duplicating engineering effort and maintenance.

Scattered data

Product catalogs, tire specs, dealer inventory and customer data lived in separate systems and disagreed across regions.

Broken experience

Customers met slow, desktop-first pages and inconsistent branding as they moved between Bridgestone properties and devices.

Slow time-to-market

With no shared platform or governance, one new capability meant rebuilding the same feature across 8+ teams.

For customers this showed up as an org chart they had to navigate. Bridgestone USA consumer, Bridgestone Canada, Motorcycle, Commercial (truck, ag and off-road) and Bandag retreads each had their own navigation, components and content. We interviewed 23 stakeholders from 11 groups, including Mobility Solutions, B2C brand marketing, consumer product, content, digital media, Canada, channel partner marketing, Motorcycle, and Bandag partner and channel marketing. Notes went into a shared synthesis board, one column per BU and one row per theme, so differences between segments were visible side by side.

Solution

Four product moves, sequenced

1 · Consolidate the platform

Migrated the fragmented B2B and B2C sites onto one scalable digital platform, retiring redundant infrastructure and workflows.

2 · Unify the data

Brought product catalogs, tire specs and dealer data into shared sources, so every brand and region shows the same accurate information.

3 · Go mobile-first

Rebuilt tire discovery, dealer locator and fleet journeys around mobile responsiveness, progressive disclosure and faster loads.

4 · Align the teams

Standardized requirements, PRDs and release readiness across product, UX, engineering and regional marketing.

Decisions

Product decisions and their trade-offs

Platform first, redesign second

Why

Redesigning seven sites on seven stacks would have multiplied the problem. Consolidating first meant every later UX improvement shipped once for every brand.

Trade-off

Customers saw visible change later than a cosmetic refresh would have delivered. I phased the migration so high-traffic journeys moved first.

Fix the data before the screens

Why

The gap analysis showed each BU maintaining its own catalog, specs and dealer data, and they disagreed. A redesigned tire finder on bad data would still give wrong answers, so shared data sources came before the new journeys.

Trade-off

Data clean-up work that customers never see, ahead of visible features. I tied it to the dealer locator and tire finder launches so the value was clear.

Prioritize journeys from traffic data

Why

Across all BU sites, visitors arrived for dealers, promotions and education. Those journeys went first on the roadmap and lead the new entry experience.

Trade-off

Brand storytelling moved below the fold. Dedicated brand modules kept a stage for marketing, and we tested that users could still reach them.

One IA with an audience switch

Why

Separate B2B and B2C sites would rebuild the silos. A single IA with a Drivers / Fleet switch shares content, data and page templates while keeping journeys distinct.

Trade-off

Harder IA governance across BUs. We de-risked it with a sitemap validation workshop with BU owners and tree testing with users before any visual design.

One intake process for every BU

Why

Regional BUs still needed to ship local features. A shared intake and prioritization process across 8+ teams let them do that without breaking the global platform.

Trade-off

More process for teams used to shipping alone. Published intake rules and a regular cross-BU review kept requests moving.

Spread investment evenly across BUs

Why

The five-dimension prioritization model showed four BUs scoring close together. A shared-platform roadmap served all of them instead of the loudest one.

Trade-off

No single BU got a fully bespoke launch first. The model gave stakeholders a transparent, defensible reason.

Kumiko Design System

Bridgestone's multi-brand house design system, built from the ground up and adopted across 14 teams. Named for the Japanese woodworking craft where precise pieces interlock without nails or glue, it unifies UI design, accessibility and brand experiences across websites, e-commerce, mobile apps and portals.

Role
Product Manager · 0 to 1 build and adoption
Focus
Multi-brand theming · WCAG accessibility · design-to-code parity
Stack
Figma library · Storybook · CMS components
Live
kumiko.bridgestone.com
Problem

Every team was rebuilding the same button

Bridgestone's brands, BUs and regions shipped in parallel with no shared foundation. The same components existed in several versions, each with its own spacing, colors and states. Figma files and production code drifted apart. Accessibility was checked late in QA, if at all. Every new brand or region meant another copy of everything. There was no house system to start from, so we built one from the ground up.

What I owned as PM, end to end

  • Vision: set the vision and aligned teams around it
  • Backlog: feature descriptions, PRDs and prioritization in Jira
  • UX: design inputs, supervision of UX work, and prototyping
  • Governance: governance documentation, contribution model and versioning
  • Delivery: sprint planning, UAT and releases with design and engineering
  • Adoption: rollout and adoption across 14 teams, with stakeholder communication throughout
  • Maintenance: ongoing upkeep, deprecations and release notes
Vision

One source of truth for every Bridgestone experience

Empower Bridgestone through a single-source-of-truth enterprise UI design system that enables consistent, efficient design and development of Bridgestone experiences across the globe: websites, digital products, mobile apps, e-commerce platforms and portals.

KPIs we measured success by

Time efficiencyCost savingsSpeed to marketBrand consistencyInnovationCollaboration

Each release was judged on whether teams built faster and cheaper, shipped sooner, stayed on brand, had room to try new ideas, and worked together more.

Screenshotted every live pattern across products and grouped them: buttons, inputs, tables, alerts, cards, navigation. Duplicates became obvious when they sat on one board.

Principles

Four principles every decision was tested against

Foundation first, features second

Invest deeply in tokens, grids, typography and core controls. Trim one-off custom features so teams build on a solid base.

Guidance over control

Clear documentation lets designers and developers adopt and co-create on their own, without rigid gatekeeping.

Composability at scale

Modular building blocks combine to meet regional or BU-specific needs without reinventing components.

Inclusive by default

WCAG accessibility, mobile-first responsive behavior and localization are built into every step of design and development.

Semantic tokens in use

Components reference color.action.primary, never a hex value. A brand is a token file, so adding one takes a day, not a rebuild.

Decisions

Choices that made it adoptable

Semantic tokens over raw values

Why

Components point to roles like action.primary and surface.raised. Multi-brand theming and dark mode become a token swap instead of a fork.

Trade-off

An extra layer of naming to learn. We published a token map in Storybook and Figma so every name links to its value and usage.

One source of truth in Figma and Storybook

Why

Figma libraries are paired with documented, production-ready components in Storybook, closing the gap between design and engineering and ending redundant front-end coding across teams.

Trade-off

Keeping two libraries in step takes discipline. Parity checks became part of each component's definition of done.

Composition over configuration

Why

A card built from slots (media, title, body, actions) covers more cases than one card with thirty props, and stays easy to test.

Trade-off

Teams write a little more markup. Ready-made patterns cover the common combinations.

Accessibility as acceptance criteria

Why

Contrast ratios are checked when tokens are defined, and focus, keyboard and screen-reader behavior are part of each component's definition of done.

Trade-off

Some brand colors failed AA on white. We kept them for decoration and defined accessible action shades beside them.

Co-creation and federated governance

Why

Distributed teams across regions and tech stacks contribute back to the system while core UX patterns stay consistent. Teams extend locally, then propose upstream through intake, triage and review. Releases follow semantic versioning with a changelog.

Trade-off

Slower than letting anyone merge. Clear service levels for review kept teams from routing around the system.

Adoption

A design system only counts if teams use it

Start where it hurts most

Launched with the highest-use, highest-drift components and a pilot team, so the first release removed real duplicate work.

Make the right way the easy way

Documented usage, states and code side by side in Figma and Storybook, so adopting a component took less effort than rebuilding it.

Let teams give back

An open contribution path turned early adopters into co-owners and grew adoption team by team to 14.

Impact

Contrast and keyboard checks happen at the token and component level, before QA.

Multi-brand by design

A brand or regional theme is a token file on shared architecture, so a new brand starts from a working system.

Bringing every merchant type, from every partner channel, under one roof: one console to build merchant pricing and calculate fees, merchant payouts and partner residuals at scale for 800,000+ merchants and 4,000 partners.

Problem

Pricing accuracy is revenue accuracy

Worldpay served many merchant types arriving through many partners, and legacy acquisitions had left pricing spread across disconnected back-office and billing engines. Sales reps, ISO partners and operations teams configured interchange-plus rates, monthly fees and terminal charges in separate systems, much of it by hand.

The result was billing errors, misconfigured fee schedules, merchant disputes and slow, manual processes. Analysts re-keyed the same merchant on several screens, saw no preview of what the merchant would pay, and requested approvals by email. In merchant acquiring, billing errors are a major driver of churn.

What I owned as PM, end to end

  • Strategy and roadmap for merchant pricing, billing, settlement and reconciliation
  • Discovery: workflow audits, stakeholder interviews, funnel and support-ticket analysis
  • Definition: Figma prototypes, epics, user stories and acceptance criteria
  • Rules: margin and approval thresholds, co-owned with finance and pricing
  • Delivery: backlog refinement, sprint planning, UAT, defect resolution and release readiness
  • Outcomes: post-launch KPIs for accuracy, efficiency and adoption
Goal

What the unified console had to fix

End fragmented rate setup

One interface for sales, partners and operations to build and quote merchant pricing, in place of several legacy back-office systems.

Cut billing and boarding errors

Standard fee templates, with approved quotes synced straight into merchant boarding and billing instead of being re-keyed.

Speed deals with governance

Real-time margin thresholds and automated approvals that stop underpriced deals and speed merchant onboarding.

    Decisions

    Design reasoning and trade-offs

    Progressive disclosure

    Why

    Most merchants fit a template by category. Advanced fee structures sit behind an expandable section, so standard setups stay fast and uncluttered.

    Trade-off

    Power users take one extra click for complex deals. The section shows a count of non-default fees so nothing is hidden by surprise.

    Error prevention over correction

    Why

    Real-time margin guardrails stop invalid quotes before submission. Catching a below-floor markup in the form is far cheaper than a back-office rejection after go-live.

    Trade-off

    Hard blocks can frustrate sales on edge-case deals. Only below-cost pricing is blocked; anything else routes to a person for approval.

    Rules-based approval routing

    Why

    Thresholds in the rules engine decide who must approve: pricing managers for low markups, partner ops for high residuals. Everything else is approved automatically with an audit trail.

    Trade-off

    Thresholds need owners and regular review. Finance and pricing leads co-own them, and every rule is versioned.

    Show the fee in dollars

    Why

    Sales and merchants think in "what do I pay on a $100 sale", not basis points. The preview speaks that language and breaks out interchange, assessment and markup.

    Trade-off

    A preview can be mistaken for a quote. It is labeled as an estimate on a sample transaction.

    Related launches

    Two problems the console surfaced, and what I shipped

    Partner payouts

    Payout transparency dashboard

    Situation
    Partner support tickets and reconciliation disputes were rising. Partners couldn't see how payouts, fees and adjustments were calculated, and many reconciled by hand in Excel.
    Research
    Analyzed ticket themes, then interviewed partners and account managers. The gap was trust and visibility, not a missing report. Finance teams needed auditable detail; operations teams needed quick summaries and issue flags.
    Making the case
    Leadership favored work tied directly to revenue. I quantified ticket trends and the hours account managers spent pulling data, and framed it as a trust and retention risk across 4,000+ partners and $500M+ in volume. Real partner quotes made it tangible.
    Lean MVP
    Payout summaries, transaction-level drill-down and downloadable reports, reusing existing data pipelines with engineering. Validated with finance, support and account management, then rolled out to a subset of partners with tracked KPIs.
    Result
    Fewer support tickets and reconciliation disputes, ~20% better operational efficiency, and more partner self-service with less reliance on internal support.

    Merchant funding

    RTP Faster Funding

    Situation
    Merchants waited for funds, especially over weekends and holidays, which hurt day-to-day cash flow.
    Research
    Settlement data showed delays concentrated in batch cycles. Segmenting merchants showed small and mid-sized retail, hospitality and service businesses hit hardest. Interviews confirmed they relied on daily funds for payroll, inventory and vendors, sometimes turning to credit.
    Reframe
    From a broad payout delay to a segment-specific cash-flow problem. Market benchmarking showed growing adoption of real-time payments as a differentiator.
    Launch
    Defined and launched RTP Faster Funding with engineering and business teams, prioritized by merchant impact and scalability, starting with high-impact SMB segments in a phased rollout.
    Result
    Near real-time payouts for eligible transactions in target segments, better cash flow across an 800,000-merchant platform, and more payout transparency for 4,000+ partners, protecting $500M+ in annual volume.
